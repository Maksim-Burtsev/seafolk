When the storm comes

Denmark names its storms. The radio says who went out anyway, and who stayed tied up.

Denmark's weather service has been naming storms since 2013, and what it publishes for each one is a date. Not an hour, not a track — a date. Everything here is therefore measured against a calendar day somebody chose, which is the biggest thing standing between these charts and the weather.

Since then it has named 24 of them. Of those, 15 land near days this archive holds, and on 14 I can watch the storm itself rather than the run-up to it. Alfrida is the one I cannot: it crossed on New Year's Day in 2019, a year the archive does not hold, so all that survives is the last three days of December — which nothing here separates from New Year's Eve.

The first question is the simplest one I can ask of a day at sea: out of every hundred boats whose radio I heard, how many actually went anywhere? The bar is a nautical mile. A boat that covered one left; a boat that did not stayed put with its set switched on. Then the same count a fortnight away, when the weather was ordinary.

The fishing fleet loses ground in 13 of the 14. Under Pia it went from 50 boats in a hundred covering a mile to 6. The rest were still on the radio all through it, sitting still in a harbour somewhere.

Before the numbers, one map: the fishing fleet is not everywhere the weather is.

Cargo's pairs of dots barely separate at all. Its worst storm in the whole archive, Dagmar·Egon, took it from 87 in a hundred to 79, and under Johanne more cargo ships moved on the storm's date than a fortnight earlier. I put cargo in as the control, expecting it to give a little. It is the one fleet in here that a named storm leaves no mark on.

One storm breaks the pattern, and it is the one that was not a storm here. On Floriane's own date nothing dipped at all. The weather service named it; this archive cannot see it. Nora is quieter still: fishing, ferries and cargo all within a hair of an ordinary day, and the lines with a Danish end running 99 per cent of their usual departures. That is February, not a thinning.

The ferries are the case I find most human, because both of the obvious things are true at once: they keep sailing and they sail less. Under Malik the lines with a Danish end ran 1 557 departures over the storm's dates against 2 210 a fortnight earlier. That is 70 per cent of the timetable, on the same ships with the same crews. A cancelled sailing is a decision somebody made in an office at six in the morning; that is a whole country's worth of them.

The work boats, meaning tugs, dredgers, guard vessels and the offshore supply fleet, have a shape of their own. They lose ground in every one of the 14 storms, worst under Dagmar·Egon, and they never collapse the way the fishing fleet does or hold the line the way cargo does: a tug goes out in weather a trawler will not, and comes home before a coaster would. A fishing fleet with a contract.

Sailing boats are drawn for only 3 of the storms, which is a fact about the Danish winter, not about the data. Across the winter days in these windows, between 9 and 22 private boats cover a mile in a whole day in the entire area — a ratio built on that is one marina's afternoon. Where the fleet really is at sea, in August, September and October, a larger share of it stays in than of any commercial fleet.

That is a whole day at a time, which is as fine as an exact count goes here. To see when inside a day a fleet gives up I have to change instrument and lose some accuracy: for each hour of a storm, how much a fleet was moving divided by the same fleet at the same hour a fortnight earlier. A hundred per cent is an ordinary day. It is an honest shape and a poor ruler (halve this number and you may have halved your boats or halved your speed), so nothing below is quoted as a count of boats.

The chart opens on Knud because the data picked it: its week beforehand is the one that looks most like an ordinary week. The fishing fleet was running at 85 per cent of normal, went to 8 at the bottom of the storm, and came back. Behind it the cargo line sits at 95 per cent and does not move. Knud also pushed the start of the Silverrudder race back a day.

What the hour adds is when. By the first midnight of the date the weather service named, before any of the storm has actually happened, the fishing fleet is already below its own quiet week in 13 of the 14 storms. On Knud it is down to 52 per cent of a normal day, and has been falling for two days.

Lay the storms on top of each other and the order never changes. The fishing fleet goes first: it halves in 14 of the 14, and the middle one of those crossings falls on the storm's own first midnight. The sailing fleet crosses the same line 3 hours before the storm's own first midnight, on the storms where enough of it was out to measure. The work boats cross the same line at 9 hours and the ferries at 17. Cargo crosses it in 2 storms out of 14. A fleet that reads a forecast stops before the wind arrives. A coaster on a schedule does not stop.

Then I went looking for where the ships that stopped had gone — the part that did not work.

A fleet that stops running has to be sitting somewhere, and the obvious somewhere is a roadstead — the sheltered water outside a big harbour where ships wait their turn. I found them without a map, as squares of open sea where a lot of big ships sit still for a lot of hours, and labelled them by hand afterwards. Then I counted how full each Danish one was during the storm's own hours, against the three days before.

Nothing. Over 70 pairings of a storm with an anchorage, the middle one sits at 1.02 times the days before it. Within a fifth of no change at all sit 52 of them. The one that moves most moves the wrong way: the Copenhagen roads emptied under Otto, from about 6 ships an hour to 3. Skagen Red, the big roadstead at the top of Jutland, does fill, by about 1.12 times, and holds it all week, before the storm and after.

The honest reading is that a named storm is one day inside a week of weather, and a roadstead fills on the week's timescale rather than the day's. That elevation cannot be pinned to the hours the weather service named, and I would rather say so.

Last, the thing I wanted to see from the start: the sea itself, hour by hour.

A clip is a bad thing to argue with, so here are the same three storms as still maps: the day before, the storm, and two days after, each set of three on one colour scale.

Press play: the orange thins out ahead of the weather while the grey keeps crossing at the same rate. On Pia the clip runs into Christmas, which empties the sea a second time for an entirely different reason. It is marked on the strip, because otherwise it reads as a storm that lasted a week.

One last thing about the hours. The deepest hour of each storm is not something I was told; I work it out, as the hour of the named date at which the commercial fleet's moving traffic sits furthest below its own fortnight. Those hours land at 0 on the clock, at 21, and all over the day between, with no clustering at any time at all — which is the best evidence I have that the date really is the only thing anyone is telling me.
