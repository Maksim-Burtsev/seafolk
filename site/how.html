Seafolk — how it was made

How it was made

What is published and what never leaves this machine, the loop that read terabytes and kept gigabytes, three bugs worth telling, and the things this data simply cannot say.

The first question

No private boat is tracked

Are you tracking private boats? No. Here is exactly what that means.

A private boat's radio ID, its name and its track never leave this machine. They sit in a working table on the disk under my desk because I need them to tell one boat from another. Nothing built from that table carries them out.

What leaves is counts: how many different boats were in one patch of sea on one day, the patch being a hexagon about the size of a small island. And only when at least 5 of them were in it. Below that the patch is dropped — not rounded, not blurred. Dropped.

"Private" is wider than the radio. It covers every small-boat radio (the cheap kind, AIS Class B) and every pleasure craft carrying a big-ship radio, between 337 and 467 a year. A yacht with a better radio is still a yacht, so the rule is keyed on the boat, not the set.

A floor like that is cheap to write and expensive to keep. Here is the bill.

Of 1 445 495 patch-days, 341 092 survive: 24 per cent of the squares, carrying 82 per cent of the movement. The rule deletes a long tail of nearly empty water and keeps the harbours and the channels, which is where the sailing is.

A finer grid does not survive at all. The same floor, hour by hour, on patches of about five square kilometres (H3 resolution 7) keeps 7 per cent of the squares and 20 per cent of the movement: unpublishable and useless at once. So the private layer is coarse and daily, and only the commercial layer, which needs no floor, is fine and hourly.

The pipeline

Terabytes went in and a few gigabytes came out

The archive is a web directory of zip files, one per month up to 2024 and one per day after that — about 2.3 terabytes from 2014 onward, of which I took a little over half. The machine that read it is a Mac Mini under a desk, so keeping any of it was never an option.

It is a loop, and the loop is the design. Download one archive. Unzip it straight into ClickHouse. Count what is in it per hexagon per hour. Write the counts. Delete the archive. Take the next one.

It ran once, from empty, over a weekend: 949 archives and 1.2 terabytes down the wire in 35 hours. The database was busy for 4.4 of them. The rest was waiting for bytes.

That ratio is the lesson worth passing on. One connection to the archive gets about a third of my home line however you ask for it, so the runner keeps 8 downloads running ahead of the file it is loading while the loading stays strictly serial — one writer, one lock. That alone made the queue 3.6 times faster. The largest monthly archive then blew the memory cap mid-count, at 806 million rows; the fix was to let the grouping spill to disk past 6 gigabytes.

Out came 11 gigabytes of counts over 2 122 days: 32 billion lines of radio read, 30 billion kept, the rest dropped as navigation buoys, shore stations, the Latitude = 91 sentinel for "no fix", and positions outside the box. A usual file goes through at 2.4 million lines a second.

Three bugs

One in the database, one in the archive, one in how ships file themselves

One — the grid was mirrored

An upgrade of the database changed the argument order of the function that turns a position into a grid square. Both orders are good numbers, so nothing threw and every table downstream looked healthy: row counts right, plausible traffic in the busiest squares. The swap reflects every point on Earth across the line where latitude equals longitude, so it showed up only on a map. The test that should have caught it called the same function with the same swapped convention, and was symmetric under the mistake. The replacement test no longer asks the database anything: it pins one square, Copenhagen, computed by another library in another language, and asserts the store has rows in it. Finding it late cost a reload from scratch.

Two — a radio cannot send that many messages

A big ship's radio (AIS Class A) sends at most one position every 2 seconds. That is the radio standard, not a habit, so a day cannot hold more than 43 200 messages from one ship — a ceiling you can test against knowing nothing about shipping.

So I counted, per month, the share of ship-days above it. Before 2023 it is 0.06 per cent, the odd garbled day. From December 2023 it runs up to 1.1 per cent every single month, and the worst ship-day holds 8.3 times the ceiling. Those days did not happen: the archive is keeping some messages twice. What convinced me the test was right is that it also found a window nobody had told it about — late August and September 2015, separately known to be duplicated.

Counting heads is immune to that, since the same boat recorded twice is still one boat; counting messages is not. So the story here is told in head counts, and no chart compares message counts across 2023 or September 2015.

Three — the ferries that are not there

A ship's type is whatever the ship says it is, and the loader resolves one identity per ship per day. A ferry that broadcast "Undefined" is not filed as a ferry that day — and not filed as a gap either. It is simply absent, with no hole where it should be, which is the worst shape a missing thing can have.

In 2025, 16 per cent of the sailing days of a 487-ship ferry fleet were filed as something other than a passenger ship, and it is not spread evenly. The Esbjerg – Nordby ferry M/F FENJA, which crosses to the island of Fanø several times an hour all year, called herself "Undefined" on 365 days of 2021. In my data she made no crossings that year, because in my data she does not exist.

No query can fix that: the type is baked in at load time, and re-reading the archive would change nothing, because the archive faithfully records what the ship broadcast. So it is measured instead, and every ferry number here is a lower bound of known size.

Limits

What this data cannot say

A radio is not a boat. Over the same weeks of the year the fleet goes from 6 137 boats in 2015 to 22 773 in 2026, 3.7 times as many. Some of that is more boats and some is people fitting a radio to a boat they already had. Divide by the commercial fleet heard on the same receivers (a control that grows when reception grows) and the rise is still 3.5, so the listening explains about 6.5 per cent of the climb. Nobody publishes how many radios were sold, so the halves cannot be separated further.

The box is not Denmark. I counted a rectangle of sea, and it reaches Kiel, Flensburg, Rügen and the Dutch Wadden. German boats are 35 per cent of the small-boat fleet in it, Danish boats 24 per cent, in every loaded year. "The fleet" always means the fleet in that rectangle.

There are holes in the calendar. 2 122 days are loaded, not eleven years of them. 2016, 2017, 2019 and 2020 were never downloaded, so a storm that fell in those years did not happen as far as this project knows.

Message counts are two instruments, for the reason in the second bug above. Head counts compare across the whole archive; message counts only inside a stretch of it.

Somebody else's map agrees about where the boats are. The European marine atlas builds its own leisure-traffic density for these waters, by a different pipeline from the same kind of radio. Rank every patch of sea by busyness and the two orders agree at 0.85 out of a possible one where both of us see the same water — close, for two methods that share no code.

They disagree in two useful ways. About 10 per cent of the traffic they see falls in squares where I have nothing, nearly all at the edges of my rectangle — in the Danish core it is 1 per cent, so that gap is my scope, not my reception. The mirror image is one patch off the island of Læsø: number 9 on my list of busiest water and number 16 984 on theirs. It holds a marina, 353 boats across the month, mostly tied up. They measure the time a track spends in a square, and a boat at a berth draws no track, so they give it 4.1 hours. Neither is wrong: one cannot see a full marina, the other cannot tell one from a busy channel.

Take it

Everything here is a repository and three commands

No number here came from anywhere but the archive, and the layout follows the trail from a chart back to its query:

sql/      every query a chart is made of
scripts/  fetch, load, export, screenshot
site/     this site: page, data module, script
notes/    the analyst charts behind the prose
docs/     the plan, the log, decisions, data card

Three commands do the work. The first fetches, counts and deletes one archive at a time, and can be stopped and restarted anywhere. The second runs any query in the repository. The third writes the open dataset and refuses to finish if the privacy test fails.

scripts/run_queue.sh queues/daily-2024-2026.txt
scripts/ch.sh sql/10_season_daily.sql
scripts/export.sh

Out of that come three files. class_a_hourly_<year>.parquet holds the commercial traffic (cargo, ferries, fishing, work boats) per small hexagon per hour, one file for each of the 8 loaded years. leisure_daily.parquet holds the private fleet per large hexagon per day, every row carrying at least 5 boats. ferry_daily.parquet holds one row per named ferry line per day, with the columns that say whether a zero means "cancelled" or "nobody heard anything".

The counts are published under CC BY 4.0; the source data is the Danish Maritime Authority's, cited as such. A permanent identifier is pending — until it exists, the repository is the address.

Source: Danish Maritime Authority, aisdata.ais.dk, used under the Danish public-data terms. Positions of private boats are never published here, at any grain, and every published number counts at least five boats together.
