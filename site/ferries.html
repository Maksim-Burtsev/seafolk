Seafolk · chapter three

Lifelines

Every Danish island hangs on the end of a boat with a timetable. The radio archive says how well that timetable is kept.

I did not go looking for ferries. I was counting pleasure boats, and the ferries kept getting in the way — the same ships, back and forth over the same strip of water, so regular that the first time I drew them I went looking for the bug.

A ferry is easy to recognise in ship radio and hard to count. It stops, it crosses, it stops again. So I looked for a ship that lay still, moved more than a hundred metres and lay still again, with its two resting places at the two ends of a named route. That gives 5.1 million crossings on every ferry route in these waters. thirty-five of those routes keep a Danish island attached to the country.

Nobody archives an old timetable, so a day is never judged against a schedule. It is judged against the same line on the same weekday in the same half of the year, which turns the Wednesday the Anholt ferry never sails into an ordinary day instead of a cancellation.

Start with one island. Læsø lies out in the Kattegat, 90 minutes from Frederikshavn by ship, and the boat is how you get there.

The winter is not a different service, it is a thinner one: 12 crossings on a July weekday and 8 on a January one. In 2 004 days of archive that ferry has failed to sail a day it owed exactly one time: 7 January 2025, storm Floriane. Its radio was on all day in Frederikshavn and it never went above a walking pace.

Switch islands with the buttons — twelve of them — and the year looks completely different. Fur runs a ferry every few minutes and its year is a solid block. Anholt is a thin comb of single round trips. Orø has a fortnight missing in March, which is not weather but the ferry's annual visit to a yard.

The pale bands are days the archive heard nothing usable, and I draw them as unknown rather than as zero. Reading a day like that as a cancellation is the easiest mistake in this project to make, and I made it: an early draft had a line failing for three days in July, and the truth was a receiver having a bad morning.

Across all the island lines, the summer timetable and the winter timetable are sometimes the same document and sometimes not remotely.

Of the twenty-three lines in that chart, seven run as many sailings on a January weekday as on a July one. The Fur ferry, the busiest in the country, drops from 140 crossings a day to 138. At the other end the boat to Christiansø goes from 10 a day to 2, and Egholm, a 5-minute hop inside the city of Aalborg, halves from 64 to 30.

The split is not about size or about how many people live on the island. It is about what the boat is for. Fur's ferry is a road: the crossing takes 4 minutes and people take it to get to work. Christiansø's boat is a day out, and a road has no season.

The same distinction shows up in the week. On ten of the island lines a Sunday looks exactly like a Tuesday, all year. The ones that do cut back cut hard: on the Bjørnø boat a Sunday is 47 per cent of a weekday.

The thinnest lifeline in the country runs out to Anholt, alone in the middle of the Kattegat: two crossings on an ordinary day — one round trip — over 27 miles that take the ship three hours each way. In summer it sails five days of the week and in winter four, and never on a Wednesday. If you live on Anholt and have to be on the mainland on a Wednesday, you left on Tuesday.

Given all that, the lines are kept remarkably well. In a year the archive holds whole, the island fleet loses a day it owed between 1.1 and 2.8 times in a hundred, with no trend across eleven years. Svendborg to Ærøskøbing and Kragenæs to Fejø have not lost one in 2 004 days.

Most long gaps are not weather. There are 359 runs of three days or more where a line's ship sat at its berth with the radio on, the longest 32 days on the Søby to Faaborg line in the winter of 2021. That is a ship in a yard, which is why the storm question below is asked over a week either side of the storm.

The five worst are all winter storms, and the worst of those is Dagmar, which ran into Egon the next morning: over those three days in January 2015 the island lines ran 52 per cent of an ordinary day and the big lines 71.

Floriane is the cleanest picture of it. On that January day the island lines ran 76 per cent of an ordinary day and the four big lines ran 100 — the Øresund and Fehmarn ships went about their business while the small ferries stayed in. A 20-minute hop across the Øresund is a different sea from the open Kattegat.

Malik is the exception, and I am glad it is there because it stops the chart being a slogan. In that storm the big four fell to 59 per cent while the island lines held 69. Underneath a pooled number the lines disagree with each other too: Svendborg to Ærøskøbing, which has never lost a whole day, ran 44 and then 39 per cent of a usual day across Malik's two days.

The last thing I went looking for was a ship. In 2021 the battery ferry ELLEN took over the crossing from Søby on Ærø to Fynshav on Als — 10 miles of water that has not moved since.

SKJOLDNAES took about 66 minutes to get across. ELLEN takes about 60, and has held that for five years. six minutes is nothing until you are the one who has to be at work on the other side. It happened again this year on Bøjden–Fynshav, where a new hybrid took the crossing from 49 minutes to 41.

I would not read a smaller step than that as a ship. On the little line out to Livø the same boat has wandered between 20 and 24 minutes since it arrived in 2021, with nobody replacing anything and no reason I can point at. The control is PRINSESSE ISABELLA, alone on Hou to Sælvig in every year the archive holds whole, still wandering between 60 and 63 minutes.

Two things here are not measurements, and it would be dishonest to close without them.

The first is the fleet I cannot see. A ferry reaches my counts only on the days it told the world it was a passenger ship, and ships get their own type wrong constantly: in 2025, 16 per cent of the sailing days of a 487-ship ferry fleet were filed as something else. two of the three ferries between Esbjerg and the island of Fanø have called themselves "Undefined" on essentially every day of every year. They cross that water several times an hour; in my data they do not exist, which is why Fanø is missing from the winter chart.

Fast ferries go the same way — they file as high-speed craft. Rønne to Ystad, the busiest route to Bornholm, has not one July weekday in the archive on which its own ships were heard, because in summer that run is all fast ferries.

The second is the check that made me trust the rest. Operators publish this year's timetable as a web page, and I read a pile of them by hand into a file; eight routes print an actual number of departures a day. On six of those eight the crossings I counted in July 2026 match the timetable exactly — not approximately, exactly. Nothing in my code has ever seen a timetable.
