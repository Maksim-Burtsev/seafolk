A year under sail

When the Danish season starts, which day of the week it happens on, how far a small boat actually goes — and what a race day looks like from the outside.

I write software and I have never raced anything. What I have is six years of Danish ship radio, spread across eleven, on a machine under my desk, and in it every small boat in these waters that carries a set and switched it on.

One rule decides most of what follows. A boat counts on a day only if it moved — half a knot, at some point, anywhere. A radio blinking at the pontoon is a boat sitting still, and counting it as a day at sea would turn the whole chapter into a story about electronics.

The first thing I drew was not the sea. It was the calendar.

The outer edge of the season hardly moves. Over the four years the archive holds whole, that bar runs between 134 and 148 days, while the fleet inside it grew 3.6 times. The middle is a different animal: the stretch above half the peak swings from 60 days to 94, in no direction I can find. The edges of a Danish season are a calendar. Its middle is weather.

What did move is the top. In 2015 the busiest week fell on 7 August; in 2026 it fell on 17 July. That is 21 days, and it stepped earlier in every one of the six years I loaded, without once going back.

Six points in a row are not a trend, and I am not going to draw a line through them. There is also a duller explanation sitting right there: the fleet more than tripled over these years, and if the people who fitted a radio after 2015 take their holidays earlier than the ones who already had one, the top of the hill moves without a single person changing their plans. What I can say is that the direction never reverses.

A season is also a place. Here is the same year as water rather than as a calendar, and then the same July ten years apart.

The week is blunter than the year.

Saturday and Sunday are the same day to within a rounding error, and everything from Monday to Thursday is the same day as well. On an ordinary summer weekday about 1 802 boats are out; on a Saturday it is 2 340. People sail when they are not at work, which is not a discovery, but it is a useful sanity check on a dataset that could easily have been measuring antennas instead.

Friday is the interesting one. It stands above midweek, and the boats that go out on a Friday go out late: on 12 days in a hundred the radio first speaks after three in the afternoon, against 10 midweek and 7 on a Saturday. That is what leaving after work looks like. It is also an indirect measure and I will not pretend otherwise — what I see is the first message of the day, not the moment the lines came off, and a boat that sat all afternoon with the electronics on counts as an early start.

The Friday edge is shrinking, too. In 2015 a Friday ran 51 per cent more late starts than a midweek day; in 2025 it was 20 per cent. Saturday and Sunday did not move at all over the same years, which is what makes me believe the Friday change is about people rather than about receivers.

Then I asked how far these boats actually go, and got the answer that rearranged the rest of the chapter.

Nearly half of all the days a radio is on, the boat does not move. That share sits between 43 and 46 per cent in every single year, while the fleet tripled underneath it. Whatever the sets in the marina are doing — charging, testing, being forgotten — they do it at the same rate now as they did in 2015.

The days that are trips are shorter than I expected. Half of them stay inside 14 nautical miles, which is an afternoon out and back with a stop for lunch, and only 11 days in a hundred pass thirty miles. Nothing here looks like the offshore sailing I had in my head; it looks like a bay.

The distances did shift once. Between 2018 and 2021 the middle of the distribution dropped from 16.8 miles to 12.6, and it has come back only halfway since. Short trips and long trips moved together, which is what a change in who is in the fleet looks like rather than a change in what any one owner does with a Saturday.

And they all start from the same place. 83 per cent of days begin in the map square that holds a marina, and it is between 82 and 83 per cent in every year of the archive. Read that literally: the square is a couple of kilometres across, so it holds the marina, the fishing quay and the moorings across the basin. It says small boats start from harbours, which surprises nobody. The useful part is that the number does not move — the fleet tripled and the geography of a boating day stayed exactly where it was.

Everything so far is the fleet on an average day. A race is the opposite.

Silverrudder is the one event in my list that grew. Measured against the same weekday either side of it, race day ran 3.1 times the boats in 2015 and 5.7 times in 2024. In 2025 the harbour area held 206 boats on race day, against 41 on a usual September Friday.

The harbour it multiplies is a quiet one — the usual days behind those numbers hold between 12 and 63 boats — so this is a small number being multiplied, not a busy September being nudged. The other Danish races in my list do nothing of the sort: their race days sit between 1.0 and 3.0 times a usual day and wander there year after year, with no direction at all.

There is a catch in this measure, and the multi-day races show it. Across the 16 Danish race weekends in the archive, the first day averages 2.5 times a usual day and the last day 1.3; the last day is the quietest of its own event in 12 of them. Boats gather before a start and then leave from wherever they finish, so a camera pointed at the start harbour sees the arrival and misses the departure. This chart watches a harbour. It does not watch a race.

Two more things the year says, neither of which needs a picture.

Night sailing barely exists, and it is the most stable number in the chapter. 4.5 per cent of summer movement falls between ten at night and five in the morning, and across six years it never leaves the range 4.3 to 4.8. The ferries over the same summers sit at 23 per cent and swing several points around it. The ferries are the control here: they run into the night because a timetable says so, and if a change in the receiver network were moving my numbers, both lines would move together. They do not. Danish pleasure sailing is a daylight activity and has been one for as long as this archive goes back.

The other thing is the shape of a day, which this chapter cannot see at all — it counts whole days, and a day hides the hour people actually cast off. That is the next chapter.
