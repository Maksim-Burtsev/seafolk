Seafolk

I listened to 6 years of ship radio

Denmark publishes every position broadcast in its waters — the small boats too. It is the only archive of its kind I could find. Here is what is in it.

I write software. I am not a sailor and I had never read a line of ship radio before this spring. What started it was a small fact: the Danish maritime authority puts its whole radio archive on a public web server, and almost nobody seems to have looked at the pleasure boats in it.

Ships carry a radio that shouts. Every few seconds it says: this is me, this is where I am, this is how fast I am going and which way I am pointed. Anyone with an antenna can hear it. It exists so that ships in fog know about each other, and it now sits on almost anything bigger than a dinghy.

Most countries publish a slice of that and keep the rest. Finland removes the private boats before publishing. Norway leaves out the small leisure craft. Denmark publishes all of it, back to 2006, as a plain directory full of zip files.

I did not take all of it. I took 2 122 days, ending on 26 August 2026: the whole of 2015, 2018, 2021 and 2025, March 2024 onward, this year as far as the files go, and two winter months around some storms. That is 6 years' worth spread over 12 calendar years, and 32 billion lines of radio. I pushed it through the Mac Mini under my desk, deleted every line the moment it had been counted, and kept 11 gigabytes of counts.

One rule shaped everything that came after. A private boat never leaves this machine as a private boat: no radio ID, no name, no track, and every number on this page counts at least 5 boats together. Ferries and commercial ships are public, and I name them.

Act one — more and more people go to sea

The first thing I looked at was the calendar

Not the sea. The calendar. How many small boats actually leave a berth on an ordinary day, and when in the year they do it. A radio switched on at the pontoon does not count here; the boat has to have moved.

The shape never changes. It lifts in May, stands through July, falls away in September, and from November to March there is almost nobody out there. What changes is the height. In the busiest week of 2015 an average of 1 165 boats were out on any given day. In the busiest week of 2026 it was 5 178.

That is the same season with 4.4 times as many boats in it — and it is the point where I stopped trusting myself. A curve that climbs like that has an obvious second explanation, and it has nothing to do with boats: more antennas along the coast, hearing boats that were always out there.

The grey line does not climb. Over the same weeks of the same years the commercial fleet went from 10 082 ships to 10 784, which is as flat as a real-world count gets, while small boats went from 6 137 to 22 773. Denmark now hears 2.1 small boats for every commercial ship in these waters. In 2015 the same count was 0.6.

What I cannot tell you is how many boats there are. I can count the ones carrying a radio, and part of that 3.7-fold rise is people fitting a set to a boat they already had. Nobody publishes how many sets were sold, so there is no way to split the two apart.

Then I looked at where the boats are registered. That is the thing I had most wrong before I started.

German boats are 35 per cent of the fleet and Danish boats 24 per cent, and Germany has led in every year I loaded. Then come Sweden at 14 per cent, Norway at 10 and the Netherlands at 7. Whenever I say "the Danish fleet" below, what I mean is the fleet inside the box I drew, which is a different and less tidy thing.

Act two — when the storm comes

A storm is the one experiment the sea runs for you

Denmark's weather service gives its storms names. 14 of them fall on days this archive holds. A storm is a clean question: everybody gets the same warning at the same time, and then you can watch who goes out anyway.

For every day around every storm I counted two things: how many boats of each kind the radio heard at all, and how many of those went anywhere — a nautical mile or more. The second out of the first is the line below.

Amy crossed Denmark on 4 October 2025. Three days before it, 69 of every hundred fishing boats the radio heard went out. On the day of the storm, 8 did. The rest had not vanished: their radios were on all through it, sitting still in harbour. They were tied up and watching.

Cargo did not notice. On the same day 79 of every hundred cargo ships went out, against 85 on an ordinary one. And not only in Amy: over 14 storms the cargo line never once drops the way the fishing line does. A coaster with a schedule does not ask the weather for permission.

The small boats behave like the fishing fleet and not like the ships. 33 of every hundred went out the day before Amy, and 7 on the day itself.

The ferries are the case I find most human, because both of the obvious things are true at once. They keep sailing and they sail less. Through Amy the lines with a Danish end ran 1 088 departures against 1 502 over the same days a fortnight earlier — the same ships, the same crews, 72 per cent of the timetable.

The deepest storm in the archive is Pia, in December 2023, which took the fishing fleet down to 4 boats in every hundred. Pick it above and the fleet is still ashore three days later — those days are Christmas, and Christmas is not weather.

Put 14 storms on top of each other and the order is always the same.

The fishing fleet is already halved when the storm's own date begins, and on the storms where enough sailing boats were out to count, they are halved 3 hours before that. Neither waits for the weather; both read a forecast and stay in. The ferries give way 17 hours in, and only in 6 storms out of 14 — which is what waiting for the actual sea looks like. Cargo crossed that line in 2.

Act three — the archive lies, a little

Three things are wrong in here, and one of them was mine

Everything above is built on an archive I did not make, on a machine I did. Both of them left marks. The first mark is the one I would want to know about if I were reading somebody else's numbers.

A big ship's radio sends at most one position every 2 seconds. Over a day that is 43 200 messages and not one more — a property of the radio, not of the ship, and not something a busy day can change. Before 2023, 0.06 per cent of ships' days in the archive sat above that ceiling. From December 2023 onward it runs up to 1.1 per cent, every single month, and the worst day I found holds 8.3 times the ceiling.

Those ships are not lying. The archive is keeping some messages twice. Counting heads is immune to that — the same ship counted twice is still one ship — so nothing in the first two acts moves. Counting messages is not, and that is why no chart on this site counts messages across 2023.

The second mark is mine, and it is the reason I now draw a map of everything before I trust it.

It did not throw an error. It did not look wrong in a table of numbers — the columns were full, the counts were plausible, the totals matched. It looked wrong on a map, which is the entire reason to draw one. Copenhagen had moved to 13 degrees north, 56 degrees east, 6 055 kilometres away. The fix was one setting. The cost was 35 hours of reloading from scratch.

The third thing is nobody's fault. A ferry lands in my counts only on the days it told the world it was a passenger ship, and ships get their own type wrong all the time. In 2025, 16 per cent of the sailing days of a 487-ship ferry fleet were filed as something else. It is not spread evenly, either. MENJA, which works the Esbjerg – Nordby crossing to Fanø, is in the archive on 2 116 of its 2 122 days and is filed as Undefined on every one of them. In my ferry counts she does not exist.
