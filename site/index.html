Seafolk · a chart of Danish waters

Who is out on the Danish sea

Small boats, ferries, cargo and fishing, from 2 122 days of open ship radio, 2015–2026

I had no idea what was in this archive, so I counted it.

ferries and passenger ships under way

Denmark publishes the radio position reports of every ship in its waters, the small private boats included. Most countries leave the small boats out. I came across the archive while building something else, a live ship map called ais-lake, which never worked because the live feeds are patchy and late.

I didn't know what was in it. So I downloaded 2 122 days of it, ran them through ClickHouse on a Mac Mini and kept only the counts: 32 billion lines of radio in, 11 gigabytes of numbers out. Everything below comes from those counts.

One rule. A private boat never appears on its own: every number about small boats on this page counts at least 5 of them. Ferries and ships are public, and I name them.

Summer

The sea fills up in July

How many small boats actually leave a berth on an ordinary day. The shape is the same every year. The height is not: in the busiest week of 2015 about 1 165 boats went out a day, in 2026 5 178.

That is 4.4 times the boats in the same season. My first guess was more antennas along the coast, hearing the same boats better.

The big ships settle it. A new antenna hears a coaster as well as a yacht, and over the same weeks the ships went from 10 082 to 10 784. The small boats went from 6 137 to 22 773.

One catch: I count boats that carry a radio, and part of the rise is people fitting a set to a boat they already had. Nobody publishes how many sets were sold.

The thing I had most wrong before I started was the flags.

Everyone else

Who shares the water

The small boats are a fraction of what is out there. I drew each of the other fleets on its own sheet, the way I would mark up a chart.

The day

Sailing boats keep office hours

Sailing boats move most around noon, and only 4.6 per cent of their movement happens at night. The rest of the sea doesn't sleep: 21 per cent of the ferries' movement and 33 per cent of the fishing boats' happens between ten at night and five in the morning.

Ferries

The battery ferry is six minutes faster

On Ærø the electric ferry ELLEN took over the crossing from Søby to Fynshav. The ship before her took 66 minutes on a typical crossing. ELLEN takes 60.

Storms

Who stays in when a storm comes

Denmark's weather service names its storms, and 14 of them fall on days I have. Everyone gets the same warning at the same time, which makes a storm a fair test of who goes out anyway.

Three days out, 55 of every hundred fishing boats went out. On the storm's own day, 16. Their radios stayed on the whole time; they were tied up in port. Cargo went from 84 to 82. A coaster with a schedule doesn't ask about the weather.

The archive's own mistakes

Two things in the archive are wrong

A ship's radio sends at most one position every 2 seconds, which is 43 200 a day. Before 2023, 0.06 per cent of ships' days held more than that. From December 2023 it is up to 1.1 per cent, and the worst day holds 359 899 messages for one ship. The archive keeps some messages twice. A ship counted twice is still one ship, so I count ships and never messages.

The second one is a filing error. A ferry lands in my ferry counts only on the days it says it is a passenger ship, and 16 per cent of the ferry fleet's days say something else. MENJA, the Fanø ferry, is filed as Undefined on 2 116 days. In my ferry counts she doesn't exist.

How it was made

One file at a time

949 zip files, 1.2 terabytes, each downloaded, counted and deleted before the next, at about 2.4 million lines a second. The whole reload took 35 hours, most of it waiting for the download.

The counts are open: the aggregated dataset is on Hugging Face under CC BY 4.0, and the code that made every figure on this page is on GitHub, with the map of every month.
