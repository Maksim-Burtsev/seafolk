Seafolk · a chart of Danish waters

I listened to 6 years of ship radio

Denmark publishes every position broadcast in its waters — the small boats too. It is the only archive of its kind I could find. Here is what is in it.

ferries and passenger ships under way, each drawn from its own radio
Who

I write software for a living. I am not a sailor and I am not a data scientist. I just like the sea and ships, and I like ClickHouse, and this project is what happens when those meet.

August 2026

It started with a different project. In late August 2026 I tried to build ais-lake, a live map of real ships with their voyages and port waits on top. It did not work out, and the reason was the data. The open real-time feeds are partial. Most of the good coverage sits in communities where you have to run your own receiver and share its feed to get in, and what you do get arrives late and with holes, so the map shows the sea in a state it is not in. No good source, no product.

The find

While I was digging through sources for it I found Denmark. Every ship carries a radio that shouts every few seconds: this is me, this is where I am, this is how fast I am going. Anyone with an antenna can hear it. Most countries publish a slice of that and keep the rest. Finland removes the private boats, Norway leaves out the small leisure craft. Denmark publishes all of it back to 2006, small private boats included, as a plain directory of zip files.

The haul

So, out of pure interest: download it, push it through ClickHouse on my Mac Mini and see what falls out. I did not take all of it. I took 2 122 days, ending on 26 August 2026: all of 2015, 2018, 2021 and 2025, March 2024 onward, this year as far as the files go, and four winter months with storms in them. That is 6 years' worth spread over 12 calendar years, or 32 billion lines of radio. It came down as terabytes of zip files, and every file was deleted as soon as it was counted. What stayed is 11 gigabytes of counts.

The rule

One rule shaped everything that came after. A private boat never leaves this machine as a private boat: no radio ID, no name, no track, and every number on this page counts at least 5 boats together. Ferries and commercial ships are public, and I name them.

Act one — more and more people go to sea

The first thing I looked at was the calendar

How many small boats actually leave a berth on an ordinary day, and when in the year they do it. A radio switched on at the pontoon does not count here; the boat has to have moved.

The shape never changes. It lifts in May, stands through July, falls away in September, and from November to March there is almost nobody out there. What changes is the height. In the busiest week of 2015 an average of 1 165 boats were out on any given day. In the busiest week of 2026 it was 5 178.

That is the same season with 4.4 times as many boats in it — and it is the point where I stopped trusting myself. A curve that climbs like that has an obvious second explanation, and it has nothing to do with boats: more antennas along the coast, hearing boats that were always out there.

The grey line does not climb. Over the same weeks of the same years the commercial fleet went from 10 082 ships to 10 784, which is as flat as a real-world count gets, while small boats went from 6 137 to 22 773. Denmark now hears 2.1 small boats for every commercial ship in these waters. In 2015 the same count was 0.6.

What I cannot tell you is how many boats there are. I can count the ones carrying a radio, and part of that 3.7-fold rise is people fitting a set to a boat they already had. Nobody publishes how many sets were sold, so the two cannot be split apart.

Then I looked at where the boats are registered. That is the thing I had most wrong before I started.

German boats are 35 per cent of the fleet and Danish boats 24 per cent, and Germany has led in every year I loaded. Then come Sweden at 14 per cent, Norway at 10 and the Netherlands at 7. Whenever I say "the Danish fleet" below, what I mean is the fleet inside the box I drew, which is a less tidy thing.

Who else is out there

Small boats share this water with everybody else, and I had no real picture of everybody else until I drew them. Each map below is a month or a year of one kind of ship under way, counted on the same patches of sea.

Act two — when the storm comes

A storm is the one experiment the sea runs for you

Denmark's weather service gives its storms names. 14 of them fall on days this archive holds. A storm is a clean question: everybody gets the same warning at the same time, and then you watch who goes out anyway.

For every day around every storm I counted how many boats of each kind the radio heard, and how many of those went a nautical mile or more. The second out of the first is the line below.

Across every one of them the shape repeats. Three days out, 55 of every hundred fishing boats the radio heard went out. On the storm's own day, 16 did. The rest had not vanished: their radios were on all through it, sitting still in harbour. They were just tied up in port.

Cargo did not notice. On the same day 82 of every hundred cargo ships went out, against 84 three days earlier, and no single storm's own line falls the way the fishing lines do. A coaster with a schedule does not ask the weather for permission.

The deepest of them is Pia, in December 2023, the lowest pale line on the chart: it took the fishing fleet down to 3 boats in every hundred. It is still low three days later, and those days are Christmas, and Christmas is not weather.

Lay the fleets side by side and the order never changes.

Of every hundred fishing boats that would have gone out on an ordinary day, 63 stayed in. 2 of every hundred cargo ships did. Hour by hour the sailing boats go in earliest of all: where enough of them were out to count, the fleet halves 3 hours before the storm's own first midnight. They go by the forecast.

The ferries are the case I find most human, because both of the obvious things are true at once. They keep sailing and they sail less. Over the storms' own days the lines with a Danish end ran 23 954 departures against 27 536 over the same days a fortnight earlier, the same ships, the same crews, 87 per cent of the timetable.

Act three — the archive lies, a little

Two things in here are wrong, and both change what you may count

Everything above is built on an archive I did not make. The first of the two is the one I would want to know about if I were reading somebody else's numbers.

A big ship's radio sends at most one position every 2 seconds. Over a day that is 43 200 messages and not one more, a property of the radio that no busy day can change. Before 2023, 0.06 per cent of ships' days in the archive sat above that ceiling. From December 2023 onward it runs up to 1.1 per cent, every single month, and the worst day I found holds 359 899 messages for one ship — 8.3 times what the radio can send.

Those ships are not lying. The archive is keeping some messages twice. Counting heads is immune to that, since the same ship counted twice is still one ship, so nothing in the first two acts moves. Counting messages is not, so no chart on this site counts messages across 2023.

The second thing is a filing error. A ferry lands in my counts only on the days it told the world it was a passenger ship, and ships get their own type wrong all the time. In 2025, 16 per cent of the sailing days of a 487-ship ferry fleet were filed as something else. It is not spread evenly, either. MENJA, which works the Esbjerg – Nordby crossing to Fanø, is in the archive on 2 116 of its 2 122 days and is filed as Undefined on every one of them. In my ferry counts she does not exist.

What is here

All of it is in a repository, and every chart is a query away

The counts, the queries that made them, the code that drew these charts and an open dataset of the results are on GitHub, under a licence that lets you do what you like with them.
