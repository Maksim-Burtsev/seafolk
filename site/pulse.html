Seafolk · chapter two

The sea by the hour

Four fleets share the same water and keep four completely different clocks. One of them is a daily routine. Three of them never close.

Small boats heard in July 2025, from the open dataset: the dots are placed at random inside their patch, and a patch is drawn only where at least five boats were heard. The rose is the sailing boats' summer day from the chart below · built by scripts/charts_pulse.py

The archive

I write software. I am not a sailor, and until this summer I had never read a line of ship radio. What got me here was a directory of zip files: the Danish maritime authority publishes every position broadcast in its waters, back to 2006, small boats included. I pushed it through the Mac Mini under my desk, deleted every line the moment it had been counted, and kept the counts.

The question

Counts by the hour, which lets you ask a question that sounds trivial and is not: when is the sea busy? There is no answer, because there is no such thing as the sea.

There is a fleet of people who sail for fun, a fleet of ferries on a timetable, a fleet of cargo ships that never stops, and a fleet of fishing boats keeping hours of its own. Put their days side by side and they barely look like the same place.

The clock

Every hour on this page is local Danish time; the archive is filed in universal time and the conversion happens inside the query.

I — the day

Sailing boats

2.8times a flat hour

The sailing fleet puts 11.7 per cent of a whole day's movement into the hour around noon. By two in the morning it is down to 0.6 per cent. That is not a fleet, it is a routine: get up, have breakfast, go sailing, be home for dinner.

Cargo ships

Cargo has nothing of the kind. Its busiest hour and its quietest hour are 4.6 and 3.7 per cent of its day, which is a straight line with a wobble in it. The ferries lift a little towards five in the afternoon, which is people going home from work, and the fishing boats lift before dawn — and both lifts are small enough that you have to be told they are there.

The night

4.6per cent, sailing

The number I like best here belongs to the night. Between ten in the evening and five in the morning the sailing fleet does that much of its moving. The working fleets do between 21 and 33 per cent of theirs.

Split the sailing fleet by the day of the week and the busy hour does not move at all — not between a Tuesday and a Sunday, not from one year to the next. What moves is how sharp it is.

Folding every Tuesday onto one clock face hides the other half of the rhythm, though: a weekend only shows up across a whole week.

Sunday, at noon

Sunday puts 12.7 per cent of its movement into that one hour, against a Saturday's 11.9 and an ordinary weekday's 11.4, and Sunday takes the least of the three into the night: 4.0 per cent against Saturday's 5.1.

Six to ten at night

The evening runs the other way. 9.1 per cent of a weekday's movement falls between six and ten at night, and only 6.5 per cent of a Sunday's. A Sunday outing is squeezed into the middle of the day and finished early.

II — the week

Seven bells

Seven bells, one a day, and the two loudest are at the weekend. The biggest single hour of the sailing week is Sunday at noon, which carries 2.0 per cent of everything the fleet does in a week — 3.4 times a flat week.

The weekend

32per cent of the week

Saturday and Sunday between them take that much of the sailing week, where a fleet that sailed the same amount every day would take 29.

Cargo

The cargo line never really leaves the dashed rule. Across every hour of the week it runs from 0.48 to 0.70 per cent — a ship with a schedule sails on a Sunday, because it is a working Monday somewhere else.

Ask each fleet for its own biggest hour of the week and no two of them answer the same way.

Fishing, upside down

10per cent on a Saturday

The fishing week is the sailing week upside down. Saturday is its quietest day of all, against 18 per cent on a Tuesday. The same water, the same weekend, opposite signs.

Fishing in winter

33→25per cent in the dark

One more thing the clock faces show if you run them twice. Turn the calendar to winter and the fishing fleet moves its whole day: its busiest hour goes from five in the morning to seven in the morning, and less of its movement happens in the dark. It works the dark in summer and the light in winter, which was the opposite of what I expected until I remembered how short a Danish December day is.

Cargo in winter

Cargo does not move at all: 2 per cent of its day would have to change hours to turn one season into the other, against 11 per cent for fishing.

Sailing in winter

The sailing fleet keeps its hour and loses its edge — the winter peak is 10.7 per cent against a summer 11.7.

III — the harbour

That is the whole country at once. Zoom in on one harbour and the hour hand gets stranger.

A turnstile

59 in, 59 out

I expected a harbour to empty in the morning and fill up again in the evening. It does not do that. The arrivals peak at noon; so do the departures, at noon. Whatever this is, it is a turnstile, not a tide.

Out of thin air

87per cent

Then I looked at where the arrivals came from, and the chart stopped being about boats. Between eight in the morning and one in the afternoon, that much of everything this marina records as an arrival had no trace within about fifteen kilometres an hour earlier. Across the ten busiest marina neighbourhoods in the country the same figure runs from 53 to 89 per cent.

A small boat's radio is wired to the boat. Switch the boat on and the radio starts talking; leave the boat at the pontoon with the power off and it says nothing, and as far as this archive is concerned it is not there. So a boat whose owner turns the key at eleven on a Saturday morning appears in the harbour out of thin air, without having sailed anywhere at all.

Most of the morning rush in a Danish marina is radios waking up. The boats were already there.

The honest limit

Which is also the honest limit of this chapter. I am not counting boats in a harbour, I am counting radios that are switched on, and the two do not differ by a fixed amount. At its quietest hour one of these marinas still shows 71 per cent of the boats it shows at its busiest, and another shows 9 per cent — same summer, same kind of harbour, and an eightfold difference in how many people leave the power on overnight. The shapes on this page are real. Anything that looks like a count of boats in a berth is not.
